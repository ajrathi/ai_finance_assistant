## What is Debt-to-Income Ratio?

The Debt-to-Income (DTI) ratio compares your monthly debt payments to your gross monthly income. It's one of the primary metrics lenders use to evaluate your ability to manage monthly payments and repay debts.

**DTI = Monthly Debt Payments ÷ Gross Monthly Income × 100**

## Calculating Your DTI

**Monthly debt payments include:**
- Mortgage or rent payment
- Car loan payments
- Minimum credit card payments
- Student loan payments
- Personal loan payments
- Child support/alimony

**Monthly debt payments do NOT include:**
- Groceries
- Utilities
- Insurance premiums (usually)
- Subscriptions

**Example:**
- Monthly gross income: $6,000
- Rent: $1,500
- Car loan: $400
- Minimum credit card: $100
- Student loan: $300
- Total monthly debt: $2,300
- DTI: $2,300 ÷ $6,000 = **38.3%**

## DTI Benchmarks

| DTI Range | Assessment | Impact |
|-----------|------------|--------|
| Under 36% | Excellent | Easy loan approval, best rates |
| 36–43% | Good | Most lenders approve |
| 43–50% | Concerning | Harder to get approved |
| Over 50% | High risk | Most lenders decline |

## Front-End vs. Back-End DTI

**Front-end DTI (Housing ratio)**: Only housing costs ÷ gross income
- Most lenders want this under 28%
- Example: $1,500 rent ÷ $6,000 income = 25% (good)

**Back-end DTI (Total DTI)**: ALL debt payments ÷ gross income
- This is the more commonly referenced ratio
- Conventional mortgages typically require under 43–45%

## Why DTI Matters for Mortgage Qualification

For a conventional mortgage, lenders typically require:
- Front-end DTI: ≤ 28%
- Back-end DTI: ≤ 43%

FHA loans allow up to 57% in some cases. VA loans are more flexible.

Your DTI directly impacts how much house you can afford to buy.

## How to Improve Your DTI

**Reduce the numerator (less debt):**
- Pay down credit card balances
- Pay off small loans
- Avoid taking on new debt before a major loan application

**Increase the denominator (more income):**
- Ask for a raise
- Add a side income source
- Include all eligible income (part-time work, alimony received, rental income)

## DTI vs. Credit Score

These are separate but related metrics:
- **Credit score**: Measures your creditworthiness based on payment history, utilization, etc.
- **DTI**: Measures your current debt burden relative to income

You can have an excellent credit score but a high DTI (lots of debt but always paid on time). Most lenders require both to meet their thresholds.

## The 28/36 Rule

A traditional guideline for housing affordability:
- Spend no more than **28%** of gross income on housing costs (principal, interest, taxes, insurance)
- Spend no more than **36%** of gross income on total debt

This rule is more conservative than what most lenders technically allow, but provides a comfortable buffer.

## Key Takeaways

1. DTI = monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income
2. Under 36% DTI is generally considered good; over 50% is high risk
3. Front-end DTI covers housing only; back-end covers all debts
4. Improve DTI by reducing debt or increasing income
5. Both DTI and credit score are evaluated for major loans

*For educational purposes only. Lending criteria vary by lender and loan type.*
