## What is Inflation?

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time, which means each dollar buys less than it used to. If a grocery basket costs $100 today and $103 next year, inflation is 3%.

The U.S. Federal Reserve targets approximately 2% annual inflation as healthy for the economy.

## How Inflation is Measured

The most common measure is the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**, tracked by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. It tracks the average price changes for a basket of goods and services including:
- Food and beverages
- Housing
- Transportation
- Medical care
- Education

**Core CPI** excludes food and energy (more volatile categories) to show underlying inflation trends.

## How Inflation Affects Your Money

### Cash Loses Purchasing Power
$10,000 in cash today, at 3% inflation, will only buy what $7,441 buys today — in 10 years. Sitting in a 0% savings account means your real wealth shrinks.

### Real vs. Nominal Returns
- **Nominal return**: The stated return on an investment (e.g., 7%)
- **Real return**: Nominal return minus inflation (7% − 3% = **4% real return**)

What matters for building wealth is your **real return**, not the nominal one.

### Debt Can Be Helped by Inflation
Fixed-rate debt (like a mortgage at 3%) becomes cheaper in real terms when inflation is higher. The dollar amount you owe stays the same, but it's worth less in real purchasing power.

## Protecting Against Inflation

### Stocks
Historically, stocks have returned ~7-10% annually — above inflation. Companies can raise prices as costs rise, partially passing inflation to consumers.

### Real Estate
Property values and rents tend to rise with inflation over time.

### Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS)
U.S. government bonds where the principal adjusts with CPI. Specifically designed as an inflation hedge.

### I-Bonds
U.S. savings bonds with interest rates tied to inflation. Popular during high-inflation periods.

### Commodities
Gold, oil, and other raw materials often rise with inflation, though they're volatile.

### What to Avoid During High Inflation
- Long-term fixed-rate bonds (their fixed payments lose purchasing power)
- Cash savings in low-yield accounts

## Historical Context

U.S. inflation averages:
- 1960s–1970s: 4–13% (oil crisis era)
- 1980s: Dropped from ~13% to ~3% after Federal Reserve intervention
- 1990s–2010s: Relatively stable, ~2–3%
- 2021–2023: Surged to 7–9% post-pandemic, then declined

## The Inflation–Interest Rate Relationship

When inflation rises, the Federal Reserve typically raises interest rates to cool spending and borrowing, which in turn slows price increases. This affects:
- Mortgage rates
- Car loan rates
- Credit card APRs
- Bond yields

## Key Takeaways

1. Inflation erodes purchasing power — invest to outpace it
2. Real return = nominal return minus inflation
3. Stocks and real estate have historically beaten inflation long-term
4. Cash savings in low-yield accounts lose value in real terms
