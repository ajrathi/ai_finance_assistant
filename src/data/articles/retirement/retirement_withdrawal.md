## The Core Challenge

Accumulating a retirement nest egg is only half the challenge. The other half is making sure your money lasts through a retirement that could span 30+ years.

## The 4% Rule (Safe Withdrawal Rate)

The 4% rule, derived from the 1994 "Trinity Study," suggests that retirees can withdraw 4% of their portfolio in year one, adjust for inflation each subsequent year, and have a high probability of their money lasting 30 years.

**Example:**
- Portfolio: $1,000,000
- Year 1 withdrawal: $40,000 (4%)
- Year 2 (3% inflation): $41,200
- Year 3: $42,436
- And so on...

**Caveats:**
- Based on historical U.S. market returns (may not hold in different return environments)
- Assumes a diversified stock/bond portfolio (typically 60/40)
- 30-year horizon — longer retirements may need 3–3.5% to be safer
- Does not account for significant healthcare costs or long-term care

## Sequence-of-Returns Risk

This is the biggest risk in retirement. A portfolio that experiences large losses early in retirement has far less ability to recover than one that experiences losses later.

**Example — Same 30-year average return, different sequence:**
| Retiree A: Good early returns, bad later | Retiree B: Bad early returns, good later |
|------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| Portfolio lasts comfortably | Portfolio depletes much earlier |

Early retirement is the most vulnerable period. Strategies to manage this:
- Keep 1–2 years of expenses in cash/bonds to avoid selling stocks at lows
- Use flexible spending (reduce withdrawals in bad market years)
- Consider annuities for a floor of guaranteed income

## Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)

The IRS requires minimum annual withdrawals from Traditional 401(k) and IRA accounts starting at age 73.

**RMD Amount** = Account Balance / IRS Life Expectancy Factor

Failing to take RMDs results in a 25% penalty (reduced from 50% in 2023) on the amount not withdrawn.

Roth IRAs do NOT have RMDs during the owner's lifetime — a significant advantage.

## Withdrawal Order Strategy

Optimal withdrawal order (to minimize lifetime taxes):
1. **Required Minimum Distributions** (mandatory)
2. **Taxable brokerage accounts** (take advantage of lower capital gains rates)
3. **Traditional IRA/401(k)** (tax-deferred — delay as long as possible)
4. **Roth IRA** (tax-free — let it grow as long as possible)

This order can reduce lifetime taxes significantly, but depends on individual tax situation.

## Roth Conversion Ladder

Between retirement and age 73 (when RMDs kick in), many retirees in lower tax brackets strategically convert Traditional IRA funds to Roth IRA:

1. Retire with a mix of Traditional and Roth accounts
2. In low-income years, convert Traditional to Roth at low tax rates
3. Pay tax now, but future withdrawals are tax-free
4. Reduces future RMDs (since Traditional balance is lower)

## Bucket Strategy

A popular way to manage retirement income:
- **Bucket 1 (Cash)**: 1–2 years of expenses in savings/money market
- **Bucket 2 (Bonds/Conservative)**: Years 3–10 of expected withdrawals
- **Bucket 3 (Stocks)**: Long-term growth for 10+ years into retirement

Withdraw from Bucket 1 first. As markets rise, refill Bucket 1 from Bucket 3. In market downturns, live off Bucket 1 and 2 without selling stocks at a loss.

## Social Security Optimization

Delaying Social Security from 62 to 70 increases your monthly benefit by approximately 77%:
- Age 62: 70% of full benefit
- Full retirement age (67 for those born 1960+): 100%
- Age 70: 124% of full benefit

For most people in good health, delaying to 70 maximizes lifetime benefits.

## Key Takeaways

1. The 4% rule suggests withdrawing 4% of portfolio in year 1, adjusting for inflation
2. Sequence-of-returns risk means early retirement losses are most dangerous
3. RMDs from Traditional accounts begin at age 73 — Roth IRAs exempt
4. Optimal withdrawal order: taxable → Traditional → Roth (for tax efficiency)
5. Delaying Social Security to 70 significantly increases monthly benefits
