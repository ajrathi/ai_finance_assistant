## Bull vs. Bear: The Basics

**Bull Market**: A sustained period of rising stock prices, typically defined as a 20%+ rise from a recent low. Associated with economic growth, low unemployment, and investor optimism.

**Bear Market**: A sustained period of falling stock prices, defined as a 20%+ decline from a recent high. Often associated with economic slowdown or recession, and investor pessimism.

The terms come from how each animal attacks: bulls thrust upward with their horns, bears swipe downward with their paws.

## Historical Context (S&P 500)

### Notable Bull Markets
| Period | Duration | Gain |
|--------|----------|------|
| 1990–2000 | ~10 years | +400% |
| 2009–2020 | ~11 years | +400% (longest in history) |
| 2020–2021 | ~1.5 years | +100% (post-COVID recovery) |

### Notable Bear Markets
| Period | Duration | Decline |
|--------|----------|---------|
| 2000–2002 | ~2.5 years | −49% (dot-com crash) |
| 2007–2009 | ~1.5 years | −57% (financial crisis) |
| 2020 (COVID) | ~1 month | −34% (fastest ever) |
| 2022 | ~10 months | −25% |

## Key Characteristics

### Bull Market Signs
- Rising GDP and corporate earnings
- Low unemployment
- High consumer confidence
- Investors buying on optimism

### Bear Market Signs
- Declining corporate earnings
- Rising unemployment
- Recession fears or actual recession
- Investors selling out of fear

## Market Corrections

A **correction** is a 10–20% decline from a recent high. Corrections are more common and shorter-lived than bear markets. The S&P 500 has historically had a correction roughly every 1.5 years.

Corrections are normal and healthy — they reset overvalued prices and create buying opportunities.

## How Long Do They Last?

**Bull markets** average about 2.7 years historically, with the longest spanning over a decade.

**Bear markets** average about 9.6 months, with the S&P 500 eventually recovering to new highs in every case throughout history.

This is why long-term investors who stay invested through bear markets have historically been rewarded.

## The Emotional Cycle of Markets

Markets often follow a predictable emotional pattern:
1. Optimism → 2. Excitement → 3. Euphoria (market peak) → 4. Anxiety → 5. Denial → 6. Panic → 7. Capitulation (market bottom) → 8. Despondency → 9. Hope → 10. Relief → 11. Optimism (cycle repeats)

Understanding this cycle helps you avoid the most common mistake: **buying at euphoria and selling at panic**.

## What to Do in a Bear Market

### What NOT to Do
- Don't panic-sell (locks in losses, misses the recovery)
- Don't try to "time the bottom" (nearly impossible consistently)

### What TO Do
- Continue regular contributions (dollar-cost averaging — you buy more shares at lower prices)
- Rebalance: Buy the asset classes that fell the most to maintain your target allocation
- Review emergency fund — ensure you won't need to sell investments
- Remember historical context: Every bear market has eventually ended with new highs

## Recession vs. Bear Market

These often overlap but aren't the same:
- **Recession**: Two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth (economic definition)
- **Bear market**: Stock market decline of 20%+

Stock markets are forward-looking — they often decline before a recession is officially declared and recover before the recession ends.

## Key Takeaways

1. Bull market = 20%+ rise; Bear market = 20%+ decline from peak
2. Both are normal parts of the market cycle
3. Bear markets average ~9.6 months; bull markets average ~2.7 years
4. Staying invested through bear markets has historically been rewarded
5. Dollar-cost averaging benefits from buying more shares at lower prices during downturns
