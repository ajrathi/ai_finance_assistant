## What is the P/E Ratio?

The Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio is the most widely used metric for valuing a stock. It tells you how much investors are willing to pay for each dollar of a company's earnings.

**P/E Ratio = Stock Price ÷ Earnings Per Share (EPS)**

**Example:**
- Apple stock price: $190
- Apple EPS (annual): $6.43
- Apple P/E: 190 ÷ 6.43 = **29.5**

Investors are paying $29.50 for every $1 of Apple's annual earnings.

## What Does the P/E Tell You?

**High P/E (20+)**: Investors expect high future growth. The stock price anticipates earnings growth beyond current levels.

**Low P/E (<15)**: The stock is cheap relative to earnings — either a value opportunity or a reflection of slower expected growth, declining business, or higher risk.

**Negative P/E**: The company has negative earnings (a loss) — P/E is not meaningful.

## Trailing vs. Forward P/E

**Trailing P/E (TTM)**: Uses the last 12 months of actual earnings. Most commonly cited.

**Forward P/E**: Uses analyst estimates of next 12 months' earnings. More speculative but forward-looking.

A forward P/E lower than trailing P/E implies earnings growth is expected.

## Historical P/E Context (S&P 500)

| Period | Average P/E |
|--------|------------|
| Long-term historical average | ~16x |
| 2010–2020 average | ~18–20x |
| During dot-com bubble (2000) | ~44x |
| During COVID crash (March 2020) | ~14x |

When the overall market P/E is above its historical average, stocks are more expensive relative to earnings.

## Cyclically-Adjusted P/E (CAPE / Shiller P/E)

Robert Shiller's CAPE ratio smooths out earnings cyclicality by using 10-year average inflation-adjusted earnings. It reduces distortion from boom/bust earnings cycles.

CAPE above 25–30 has historically indicated elevated valuations. But markets can remain "overvalued" by CAPE for years.

## Industry-Specific P/E Norms

P/E ratios vary significantly by industry:

| Sector | Typical P/E Range |
|--------|------------------|
| Technology (growth) | 25–50x |
| Healthcare | 20–35x |
| Utilities (slow growth) | 15–20x |
| Banking/Financial | 10–15x |
| Energy (cyclical) | 10–20x |

Always compare a company's P/E to its industry peers, not just the market average.

## Limitations of P/E

1. **Doesn't work for negative earnings**: Loss-making companies have no meaningful P/E
2. **Accounting manipulation**: Earnings can be influenced by accounting choices
3. **Ignores debt**: A heavily indebted company may look cheap on P/E but carries risk
4. **Not useful across industries**: Tech vs. utilities P/E comparison is misleading
5. **Future-dependent**: A high P/E is justified only if future growth materializes

## Related Metrics

**PEG Ratio = P/E ÷ Expected Earnings Growth Rate**
A PEG below 1.0 suggests a stock may be undervalued relative to its growth. Developed by Peter Lynch.

**P/B Ratio (Price-to-Book)**: Price vs. book value. Useful for banks and asset-heavy companies.

**EV/EBITDA**: Enterprise Value to Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization. Better for comparing companies with different capital structures.

## Key Takeaways

1. P/E = stock price ÷ earnings per share
2. Higher P/E = investors expect more growth
3. Compare P/E to industry peers and historical averages, not in isolation
4. Trailing P/E uses past earnings; forward P/E uses estimates
5. P/E is one of many metrics — never use it in isolation

*For educational purposes only. P/E ratio is one metric and should be considered alongside many others. This is not investment advice.*
