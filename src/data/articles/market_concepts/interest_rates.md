## What are Interest Rates?

An interest rate is the cost of borrowing money, expressed as a percentage of the amount borrowed. When you take out a loan, you pay interest. When you deposit money in a savings account, the bank pays you interest.

Interest rates are set through a complex interplay of central bank policy, market forces, and economic conditions.

## The Federal Reserve (The Fed)

The Federal Reserve is the central bank of the United States. Its dual mandate:
1. **Maximum employment**: Keep unemployment low
2. **Stable prices**: Keep inflation near 2%

The Fed's most important tool is the **federal funds rate** — the target interest rate at which banks lend to each other overnight.

## The Fed Funds Rate

Although it's technically the rate banks charge each other, the federal funds rate ripples through the entire economy:

| Fed Action | Effect on Rates | Effect on Economy |
|-----------|-----------------|------------------|
| Raise rates | Higher borrowing costs | Slows spending, reduces inflation |
| Cut rates | Lower borrowing costs | Stimulates spending, may increase inflation |

## How Interest Rates Affect Your Finances

### Mortgages
The 30-year fixed mortgage rate closely follows the 10-year Treasury yield (which is influenced by, but not identical to, the Fed funds rate).
- Rate increase from 3% to 7% on a $400,000 mortgage increases monthly payment by ~$1,050

### Credit Cards
Credit card APRs typically move with the prime rate (which tracks the Fed funds rate). Most credit cards have variable rates — they increase when the Fed raises rates.

### Savings Accounts
High-yield savings accounts and money market rates rise when the Fed raises rates. During the 2022-2023 hiking cycle, HYSA rates rose from ~0.5% to 5%+.

### Bonds
Rising interest rates cause existing bond prices to fall (inverse relationship). New bonds offer higher yields, making old lower-yielding bonds less valuable.

### Stocks
The relationship between rates and stocks is complex:
- Higher rates → higher discount rate → lower present value of future earnings → lower stock prices (theoretically)
- Higher rates → economy may slow → lower corporate earnings → lower stock prices
- Growth/tech stocks with distant future earnings are most sensitive to rate changes

## The Yield Curve

The yield curve plots interest rates across different maturities (3-month, 1-year, 10-year, 30-year Treasury bonds).

**Normal yield curve**: Long-term rates are higher than short-term (investors demand more compensation for locking up money longer)

**Inverted yield curve**: Short-term rates exceed long-term rates — historically a reliable recession predictor. Has preceded every U.S. recession since 1960.

**Flat yield curve**: Rates are similar across maturities — often a transition between normal and inverted.

## The Rate Cycle

Interest rates tend to move in cycles:
1. **Low rates**: Stimulate economy after recession
2. **Rising rates**: Combat inflation as economy heats up
3. **High rates**: Slow the economy, reduce inflation
4. **Falling rates**: Respond to economic slowdown or recession
5. **Low rates** again: Repeat

Understanding where rates are in the cycle helps frame investment decisions.

## Real vs. Nominal Interest Rates

**Nominal rate**: The stated rate (e.g., 5% on your savings account)
**Real rate**: Nominal rate minus inflation (5% − 3% inflation = 2% real rate)

What matters for purchasing power is the **real rate**. During high inflation, even seemingly high nominal rates can be negative in real terms.

## Key Takeaways

1. The Federal Reserve sets the federal funds rate to balance employment and inflation
2. Higher rates slow the economy and reduce inflation; lower rates stimulate growth
3. Rising rates hurt bond prices, existing variable-rate debt, and growth stocks
4. Rising rates benefit savers (higher yields on savings accounts)
5. An inverted yield curve has historically been a reliable recession signal
