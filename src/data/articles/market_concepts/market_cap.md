## What is Market Capitalization?

Market capitalization (or "market cap") is the total market value of a company's outstanding shares of stock. It's the simplest measure of a company's size from an investor's perspective.

**Market Cap = Current Share Price × Total Shares Outstanding**

**Example:**
- Apple share price: $190
- Shares outstanding: 15.5 billion
- Apple market cap: $190 × 15.5B = **~$2.95 trillion**

## Market Cap Categories

| Category | Market Cap Range | Examples |
|----------|-----------------|---------|
| Mega-cap | >$200 billion | Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Google |
| Large-cap | $10B – $200B | Nike, Starbucks, Disney |
| Mid-cap | $2B – $10B | Many regional banks, mid-size retailers |
| Small-cap | $300M – $2B | Smaller growth companies |
| Micro-cap | $50M – $300M | Very small companies |
| Nano-cap | <$50M | Penny stocks territory |

## Why Market Cap Matters

### Risk and Return Characteristics

| Category | Growth Potential | Risk Level | Volatility |
|----------|-----------------|------------|------------|
| Large-cap | Moderate | Lower | Lower |
| Mid-cap | Higher | Moderate | Moderate |
| Small-cap | Highest | Higher | Highest |

Historically, small-cap stocks have outperformed large-caps over very long periods, but with significantly more volatility and risk.

### Index Composition

Market cap determines how companies are weighted in major indexes:
- **S&P 500**: 500 largest U.S. companies, weighted by market cap
- **Russell 2000**: 2,000 small-cap U.S. companies
- **Total Market Index**: All publicly traded U.S. companies

Because S&P 500 is cap-weighted, the largest companies (Apple, Microsoft, Amazon) have outsized influence on the index's performance.

## Market Cap vs. Enterprise Value

**Market cap** reflects equity value only (what shareholders own).

**Enterprise Value (EV)** = Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash. EV represents the theoretical takeover price of a business, including its debt.

For comparing companies with different debt levels, EV-based ratios (like EV/EBITDA) are more appropriate than market cap alone.

## Float vs. Market Cap

**Market cap** counts ALL outstanding shares.
**Float** counts only shares available for public trading (excluding insider-held, restricted shares).

For large established companies, float and market cap are close. For companies with significant insider ownership, they can differ substantially.

## Why Large-Caps Dominate Portfolios

1. **Liquidity**: Easy to buy and sell large amounts without moving the price
2. **Analyst coverage**: More information available reduces information asymmetry
3. **Stability**: Established businesses with diverse revenue streams
4. **Global recognition**: Major index and ETF inclusion drives demand

## International Market Caps

The U.S. represents roughly 60% of total global stock market capitalization. Key non-U.S. markets:
- European markets: ~20%
- Japan: ~5%
- China: ~3%
- Emerging markets combined: ~12%

International diversification exposes portfolios to different economic cycles and currencies.

## Key Takeaways

1. Market cap = share price × shares outstanding
2. Large-cap = established, stable, lower risk; Small-cap = higher growth potential, higher risk
3. S&P 500 is cap-weighted — the largest companies dominate its performance
4. Enterprise Value is more complete than market cap for comparing companies with debt
5. U.S. markets represent ~60% of global market cap
