## What is a Bond?

A bond is a loan you make to a government or corporation. In exchange, they promise to:
1. Pay you regular **interest (coupon payments)** — typically semi-annually
2. Return your original investment (**principal**) at the end of the loan term (**maturity date**)

Bonds are part of the "fixed income" asset class because the payments are predetermined.

## Bond Terminology

- **Par Value (Face Value)**: The principal amount — usually $1,000 per bond
- **Coupon Rate**: Annual interest rate expressed as a % of par value
- **Maturity Date**: When the bond expires and principal is returned
- **Yield**: Actual return if bought at current market price (may differ from coupon)
- **Duration**: Measure of a bond's sensitivity to interest rate changes

## Types of Bonds

### Government Bonds
- **U.S. Treasury Bonds**: Backed by U.S. government (considered virtually risk-free)
  - T-Bills: <1 year maturity
  - T-Notes: 2–10 year maturity
  - T-Bonds: 10–30 year maturity
- **TIPS**: Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities — principal adjusts with inflation
- **Municipal Bonds ("Munis")**: Issued by states/cities, often tax-exempt

### Corporate Bonds
- **Investment-Grade**: Rated BBB- or above (lower risk, lower yield)
- **High-Yield ("Junk Bonds")**: Below BBB- (higher risk, higher yield)

## The Bond Price–Interest Rate Relationship

This is the most important concept for bond investors:

**When interest rates rise → bond prices fall**
**When interest rates fall → bond prices rise**

Why? If you own a bond paying 3% and new bonds offer 5%, your bond is less desirable — so its price drops until its effective yield matches the market rate.

**Duration** measures this sensitivity:
- A bond with 10-year duration loses ~10% in price if rates rise 1%
- Short-term bonds are less affected by rate changes than long-term bonds

## Bond Ratings (Credit Risk)

Rating agencies (Moody's, S&P, Fitch) assess the likelihood of default:

| Rating | S&P | Moody's | Category |
|--------|-----|---------|----------|
| Highest | AAA | Aaa | Investment Grade |
| Good | BBB | Baa | Investment Grade |
| Speculative | BB | Ba | High Yield |
| Very Risky | CCC | Caa | High Yield |
| Default | D | C | — |

Lower ratings = higher yield (to compensate investors for higher risk).

## Bond Yield

**Current Yield** = Annual Coupon / Current Market Price
**Yield to Maturity (YTM)** = Total return if held to maturity, accounting for price paid

When bonds trade at a **discount** (below face value), YTM > coupon rate.
When bonds trade at a **premium** (above face value), YTM < coupon rate.

## Why Hold Bonds?

1. **Stability**: Less volatile than stocks
2. **Income**: Regular coupon payments
3. **Diversification**: Often move opposite to stocks in recessions
4. **Capital preservation**: Especially important near retirement

## Bond Funds

Instead of buying individual bonds, most investors use bond funds:
- Instant diversification across hundreds of bonds
- More liquid than individual bonds
- Professional management of duration and credit quality

## Key Takeaways

1. Bonds = loans to governments or corporations with fixed interest payments
2. Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions
3. Credit ratings reflect default risk — higher yield = higher risk
4. Bonds provide stability and income to offset stock volatility in a portfolio

*For educational purposes only. Bond investments carry interest rate risk and credit risk.*
