## What is a REIT?

A Real Estate Investment Trust (REIT) is a company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate. REITs allow individual investors to earn dividends from real estate investments without having to buy, manage, or finance properties themselves.

Think of a REIT as a mutual fund for real estate.

## How REITs Work

By law, REITs must:
- Invest at least **75% of assets** in real estate
- Derive at least **75% of income** from real estate (rent, mortgage interest)
- Pay out at least **90% of taxable income** as dividends to shareholders
- Have at least 100 shareholders

The 90% dividend payout requirement makes REITs attractive income investments but limits their ability to reinvest internally.

## Types of REITs

### By Asset Type
- **Office REITs**: Office buildings (examples: Boston Properties, SL Green)
- **Residential REITs**: Apartment complexes, single-family rentals
- **Retail REITs**: Shopping malls, strip centers
- **Industrial REITs**: Warehouses, logistics centers (growing with e-commerce)
- **Healthcare REITs**: Hospitals, nursing facilities, medical offices
- **Data Center REITs**: Server farms, data centers (digital infrastructure)
- **Mortgage REITs (mREITs)**: Own mortgages, not physical properties — higher risk, higher yield

### By Trading Method
- **Publicly Traded REITs**: Listed on stock exchanges, highly liquid
- **Non-Traded REITs**: Not on exchanges, illiquid, often higher fees — generally avoided by informed investors
- **Private REITs**: Not registered with SEC, for accredited investors only

## REIT Returns and Income

REITs have historically provided:
- **Total returns**: Similar to stocks long-term (~10% annually for equity REITs)
- **Dividend yields**: Typically 3–5% (much higher than S&P 500 average of ~1.5%)

REITs offer:
1. Dividend income (quarterly or monthly)
2. Potential share price appreciation
3. Inflation hedge (real estate values and rents tend to rise with inflation)

## Tax Treatment of REIT Dividends

REIT dividends are mostly ordinary income (not qualified dividends), making them less tax-efficient than most stocks. Consider holding REITs in tax-advantaged accounts (IRA, 401k) to avoid the tax drag.

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (2017) allows a 20% deduction on "pass-through income" including REIT dividends, reducing the effective tax rate somewhat.

## REITs in a Portfolio

REITs have low correlation with stocks and bonds, making them a useful diversifier. Many financial advisors suggest 5–15% of a portfolio in REITs.

Many total market and international index funds already include REITs proportionally. If you use a total market fund, you may already have REIT exposure.

## Key Metrics for Evaluating REITs

**Funds from Operations (FFO)**: Better than earnings for REITs because it adds back depreciation (real estate generally doesn't depreciate like accounting suggests)

**FFO Yield**: FFO per share ÷ share price (higher = potentially undervalued)

**Net Asset Value (NAV)**: Estimated value of underlying properties per share

**Occupancy Rate**: Percentage of properties leased — higher is better

**Debt-to-Equity**: REITs typically carry more debt than other companies (real estate is capital-intensive)

## REIT ETFs

Rather than picking individual REITs, most investors use REIT ETFs for instant diversification:

- Vanguard Real Estate ETF (VNQ): Broad U.S. REIT exposure, low cost
- iShares Global REIT ETF (REET): International + U.S. exposure

## Key Takeaways

1. REITs own income-producing real estate and must pay 90%+ of income as dividends
2. Publicly traded REITs offer real estate exposure with stock-like liquidity
3. Strong dividend yields (3–5%) make them attractive income investments
4. Hold REITs in tax-advantaged accounts due to their ordinary income tax treatment
5. REIT ETFs provide diversification across property types and geographies

*For educational purposes only. REITs carry market risk, interest rate risk, and sector-specific risk. All investments can lose value.*
