## What is Asset Allocation?

Asset allocation is the process of dividing your investment portfolio among different asset classes — primarily stocks, bonds, and cash. It's the single most important decision in investing, accounting for over 90% of portfolio performance variation according to academic research.

## The Three Main Asset Classes

| Asset Class | Expected Return | Risk | Time Horizon |
|-------------|-----------------|------|--------------|
| Stocks (Equities) | 7–10% annually | High | 5+ years |
| Bonds (Fixed Income) | 3–5% annually | Medium | 2–10 years |
| Cash/Money Market | 1–4% annually | Very Low | <2 years |

## Why Asset Allocation Matters More Than Stock Selection

The famous Brinson, Hood, and Beebower study (1986) found that asset allocation policy explained over 90% of the variation in portfolio returns. In other words, whether you own stocks and bonds matters far more than which specific stocks you own.

## Risk Tolerance: The Starting Point

Your ideal allocation depends on:

1. **Time horizon**: How long until you need the money?
   - 20+ years: Can afford more stocks (time to recover from downturns)
   - 5–10 years: Moderate mix
   - <5 years: More conservative (bonds, cash)

2. **Risk capacity**: Can you financially handle a 40% drop?
3. **Risk tolerance**: Can you emotionally handle a 40% drop without panic-selling?

## Common Allocation Approaches

### Age-Based Rule of Thumb
**"110 minus your age" in stocks**:
- Age 30: 80% stocks, 20% bonds
- Age 50: 60% stocks, 40% bonds
- Age 70: 40% stocks, 60% bonds

Some use "120 minus age" given longer life expectancies.

### Target-Date Funds
Automatically adjust allocation as you approach a target retirement year. They start aggressive (high stocks) and gradually become conservative (more bonds). Simple "set it and forget it" approach.

### The Three-Fund Portfolio
Popular approach with Bogleheads:
1. U.S. Total Stock Market (e.g., 60%)
2. International Stock Market (e.g., 20%)
3. U.S. Bond Market (e.g., 20%)

Adjust percentages based on risk tolerance.

## Rebalancing

Over time, your allocation drifts as different assets grow at different rates. **Rebalancing** restores your target allocation by selling assets that have grown above target and buying those below target.

**Example:** Target: 60% stocks, 40% bonds
- After a bull market: portfolio drifts to 75% stocks, 25% bonds
- Rebalance: Sell some stocks, buy bonds to return to 60/40

Rebalance annually or when any asset class drifts more than 5% from target.

## Risk-Based Portfolio Examples

**Aggressive (Long time horizon, high risk tolerance)**:
- 90% stocks, 10% bonds
- Expected volatility: High (could drop 40–50% in a crash)
- Expected return: Higher long-term

**Moderate**:
- 60% stocks, 40% bonds
- The traditional "balanced" portfolio

**Conservative**:
- 30% stocks, 70% bonds/cash
- Appropriate for those near or in retirement

## Common Allocation Mistakes

1. **Too conservative too young**: Missing decades of growth
2. **Too aggressive near retirement**: A market crash right before retiring can be catastrophic
3. **Ignoring international**: U.S. market doesn't always lead
4. **Not rebalancing**: Drift leads to unintended risk levels

## Key Takeaways

1. Asset allocation is the most important portfolio decision
2. Higher stocks = higher expected returns and higher risk
3. Align allocation with time horizon and risk tolerance
4. Rebalance annually to maintain target allocation
5. As retirement approaches, gradually reduce stock exposure

*For educational purposes only. Asset allocation does not guarantee profit or protect against loss.*
