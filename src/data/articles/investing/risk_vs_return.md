## The Core Principle

In investing, there is a fundamental trade-off: **higher potential returns require accepting higher risk**. There is no free lunch — if someone offers you high returns with no risk, it's likely a fraud.

Risk and return are two sides of the same coin.

## What is Investment Risk?

Investment risk is the uncertainty of outcomes — specifically, the possibility that your actual return will differ from your expected return. This includes:

- **Losing money** (downside risk)
- **Underperforming alternatives** (opportunity cost risk)
- **Not keeping up with inflation** (inflation risk)
- **Not having money when you need it** (liquidity risk)

## Types of Risk

### Market Risk (Systematic Risk)
The risk that the entire market declines (recession, global events). Cannot be eliminated through diversification.

### Company-Specific Risk (Unsystematic Risk)
The risk that a specific company fails or underperforms. **Can be eliminated through diversification.**

### Interest Rate Risk
When rates rise, bond prices fall. Longer-duration bonds are more sensitive.

### Inflation Risk
Returns that don't outpace inflation result in real loss of purchasing power.

### Liquidity Risk
Cannot sell quickly at a fair price. Real estate and private investments carry this risk.

### Credit Risk
Bonds may not pay if the issuer defaults. Higher for corporate vs. government bonds.

## Measuring Risk: Standard Deviation and Volatility

**Standard deviation** measures how much returns vary around the average:

| Asset | Avg. Annual Return | Standard Deviation |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------|
| U.S. Stocks | ~10% | ~15–20% |
| Bonds | ~4–5% | ~5–8% |
| Cash | ~2–3% | ~1–2% |

A higher standard deviation means wider swings in value — more risk, but potentially more reward.

## The Efficient Frontier

Modern Portfolio Theory (Markowitz, 1952) shows that for any given level of risk, there is an optimal portfolio that maximizes expected return. Portfolios on the **efficient frontier** offer the best possible return for their risk level.

Key insight: Combining assets with low correlation reduces portfolio risk without proportionally reducing expected return.

## Risk-Adjusted Returns

Pure returns don't tell the full story — you need to consider what risk was taken to achieve them.

**Sharpe Ratio** = (Portfolio Return − Risk-Free Rate) / Standard Deviation

A higher Sharpe Ratio means better return per unit of risk taken. Two funds with the same return but different volatility — the less volatile one has a higher Sharpe Ratio and is a better risk-adjusted investment.

## Risk Tolerance vs. Risk Capacity

**Risk tolerance**: How much volatility you can emotionally handle (watching your portfolio drop 40% without panic-selling)

**Risk capacity**: How much volatility you can financially handle (you won't need this money for 20 years = high capacity)

Both must be considered. High capacity but low tolerance leads to panic-selling at the worst time.

## Historical Risk-Return Comparison

| Investment | 30-Year Avg. Return | Worst Single Year |
|------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| U.S. Stocks | ~10% | −43% (1931) |
| International Stocks | ~8% | −45% (2008) |
| Bonds | ~5% | −15% (2022) |
| Cash | ~3% | Barely positive |

Stocks have the highest long-term returns AND the highest short-term volatility.

## Risk and Time Horizon

Over short time periods, stocks are highly unpredictable. Over long periods (20+ years), they have historically always been positive.

- **1-year S&P 500 return**: Ranges from −43% to +54%
- **20-year S&P 500 return**: Has always been positive historically

This is why time horizon is so critical in determining appropriate risk level.

## Key Takeaways

1. Higher potential returns require accepting higher risk — no exceptions
2. Unsystematic (company-specific) risk can be eliminated through diversification
3. Risk tolerance (emotional) AND risk capacity (financial) both matter
4. Longer time horizons allow for more risk because you can recover from downturns
5. Sharpe Ratio measures return per unit of risk — useful for comparing investments
